\title{Digital Twin Fidelity for Networked IoT Services}
\author{Shreyas Udaya, Sreya Viswanadha, Aiden Wiehn, Leyla Nazhandali}
\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Digital twins provide estimates of physical systems to networked Internet of Things (IoT) services, but the information available at the receiver may differ from the estimates generated at the source due to communication delays and state availability. We present a service-relative fidelity framework that evaluates receiver-held states against service-specific physical accuracy, freshness, and evidence requirements. By keeping source estimates fixed and varying delivery conditions, the framework identifies services that recover with improved communication and those that remain unqualified under the tested delivery conditions. We also use historical recordings to screen candidate service–delivery settings and reference-based offline optimization to assess feasibility and minimize transmissions.

Across ten mobile-robot recordings, improved delivery restored 27 of 30 recoverable cases under source-time centered estimates. However, introducing a 100-ms availability delay reduced restoration to 17 of 31 cases, while separately trained past-only estimates restored 27 of 29. At a 25 \% target acceptance level, historical screening incorrectly accepted settings that failed their service requirements at rates of 2.0\%, 21.2\%, and 11.3\%, respectively, across these three configurations. Offline planning reduced median transmissions by 31.4\% for buffered-centered estimates and 12.2\% for past-only estimates compared with qualifying fixed-rate delivery. These results demonstrate how receiver-side evaluation can distinguish failures that communication improvements can resolve from those that persist, while highlighting the importance of state availability when assessing service adequacy and communication decisions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
\revision{Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT) systems connect physical
assets to edge and cloud services through sensing, state estimation, and
communication. Digital twins use this information to support monitoring
and decisions. A service, however, acts on the state that has reached it,
which can differ from the twin's latest estimate. Evaluating the source
model alone therefore leaves an operational question unanswered: does the
information available to this service meet its requirements, and would
changing delivery restore those requirements if it does not?}

\secondrev{The service path comprises a source twin, a network, and a
receiver hosting the consuming application. For example, a robot twin
may send position, heading, and speed estimates from an on-robot or edge
processor to a remote monitoring service. We call the destination the
\emph{receiver} and the consuming application the \emph{service}. The
receiver uses the newest estimate that has arrived, so delivery can
change service adequacy even when the estimator remains unchanged.}

Whether a received state is useful depends on the service. A service that evaluates recent motion may tolerate some accumulated error in absolute position, while a service that needs the robot's current location may not. Conversely, a state may be accurate for the time it represents but too old to support a time-sensitive decision. A single trajectory-accuracy value therefore cannot, by itself, establish whether the information at the receiver is suitable for every service.

This distinction matters when diagnosing a service failure. If the source estimates meet the service's physical-error requirements but arrive too late, improved delivery may help. If the service still fails under the best tested delivery, delivery changes alone have not restored it. Missing reference measurements, poor synchronization, or inadequate data quality can also make the outcome unobservable. Keeping these outcomes separate shows what the evidence supports and which intervention is relevant.

\secondrev{Trace alignment evaluates correspondence between physical and
digital behavior \cite{munoz2024trace}, while Age of Information
characterizes the freshness of received updates \cite{yates2021aoi}.
We connect physical discrepancy, freshness, and availability of evaluation evidence
through explicit service requirements applied to the receiver-held state.
Holding the source estimates fixed then makes it possible to test whether
delivery changes restore qualification and to bound the communication
needed by qualifying schedules.}

We address this question with a service-relative evaluation framework. Each service declares the state quantities it needs, the time horizon over which they are evaluated, acceptable physical discrepancy, a maximum state age, and the evidence required for evaluation. The framework reports whether observable cases meet both the physical and freshness requirements, and marks cases with insufficient evidence as \emph{unobservable}. It also builds a qualification surface from prior physical sequences to screen service--delivery regimes for consideration; the surface is an offline guide, not a per-state accuracy check.

\secondrev{The framework supports three decisions. \emph{Qualification}
determines whether the state received under a delivery condition meets a
service contract. \emph{Delivery recovery} tests whether a failed service
qualifies when delivery improves, with its source estimates and
requirements unchanged. \emph{Offline planning} bounds achievable
qualification and minimizes transmissions within a specified schedule
family. Together, these decisions connect source quality to the network
resources needed by a consuming service.}

\revision{We evaluate ten i2Nav recordings \cite{tang2025i2nav} with particular attention to when source estimates become available for delivery. Some estimates rely on future sensor samples and therefore cannot be transmitted immediately at their source timestamps. To examine the effect of this delay, we compare three configurations: centered estimates available at their source
timestamps, the same estimates buffered by 100~ms, and separately trained
past-only estimates. This design separates delayed availability from
changes to the estimated trajectory. At the 25\% acceptance request,
the historical screen falsely qualifies 4/200, 33/156, and 19/168
accepted settings, respectively. Each accepted set contains local-motion
services. The contrast shows that source quality, availability, and the
requested service jointly determine the usefulness of historical
screening. Paired delivery tests, measured Linux \texttt{tc netem}
arrivals, offline planning, and additional robot and thermal studies
examine the corresponding evaluation and intervention decisions.}
The contributions of this work are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
    \setlength{\parsep}{0pt}
    \setlength{\topsep}{2pt}
    \item We develop a service-relative digital twin fidelity framework that evaluates the state available to a network receiver against a service's physical-error, freshness, and evidence requirements. The framework distinguishes qualified, unqualified, and unobservable outcomes without collapsing position error, heading error, and state age into a single score.

    \item We introduce a paired delivery-remediability analysis that holds the source twin and its recorded estimates fixed while changing delivery conditions. This identifies whether improved delivery restores a failed service or whether it remains unqualified under the tested ideal-available delivery.

    \item \revision{We evaluate qualification transfer across held-out
    physical recordings, compare simple and learned screening rules, and
    isolate source-state availability through a controlled buffered replay.
    Sampled delivery, measured Linux \texttt{tc netem} traces, and supporting
    robot and thermal studies test complementary parts of the framework.}

    \item \new{We develop reference-based offline delivery planning with
    an optimistic qualification bound and transmission minimization.
    The bound identifies cases \rev{no fully observable schedule in the
    declared bounded-age class can restore},
    while optimization finds minimum or near-minimum-transmission solutions within a
    specified family of rate-block schedules.}
\end{enumerate}
\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

Digital-twin evaluation builds on verification, validation, and
model-credibility. These approaches assess a digital representation
against its physical counterpart and the use it is intended to support
\cite{jones2020characterising,shao2023credibility,bitencourt2025vv}.
This matters because aggregate resemblance alone does not establish that
a twin's reported state is adequate for the decisions made by consuming
applications.

Prior research has developed methods for comparing physical and virtual
behavior. Su \emph{et al.} characterize digital-twin identicality using
properties including trueness, precision, completeness, and latency
\cite{su2023identicality}. Mu{\~n}oz \emph{et al.} use trace alignment to
compare physical and digital executions \cite{munoz2024trace}. For mobile robots,
absolute and relative pose error metrics summarize global trajectory
error and local motion error, respectively \cite{sturm2012rgbd}. These
approaches provide important ways to assess model behavior. Our work asks
a related but distinct receiver-side question: does the state that has
actually arrived satisfy the requirements of the specified service?

This question also connects digital twin evaluation to information
freshness in networked systems. Age of Information (AoI) measures how
long it has been since the freshest available update was generated
\cite{kaul2012realtime,yates2021aoi}. Extensions consider the
application level usefulness of information or its freshness when a
query is made \cite{maatouk2023aoii,chiariotti2022query}. Research on
digital twin synchronization similarly studies how update and
communication decisions affect the relationship between physical and
virtual states. \new{Tan and Matta balance synchronization and prediction-bias
costs \cite{tan2024synchronization}. These
Li et al. minimize state staleness
under update budgets and support fidelity-aware queries
\cite{li2025budget}. Our controlled delivery comparison freezes the
generated estimates and tests whether changing their arrival history
restores a declared service contract.} \new{Kim et al. use one-slot
state predictions, spatial urgency, and Age-of-Information fairness to
schedule synchronization dynamically across multiple physical objects
\cite{kim2026prediction}. Unlike prior synchronization approaches, our framework jointly evaluates receiver-side physical accuracy, freshness, and evidence availability to assess service qualification and delivery recovery. Delivery-planning analysis instead uses
recorded reference data to test whether schedules meet declared physical-
error and freshness requirements, and to measure their transmission
cost. It provides an offline benchmark for future online service-aware
scheduling.} The receiver's information can be fresh but physically
inaccurate, or physically accurate at generation but too old when used.

Selective prediction evaluates error among accepted cases together with
acceptance coverage \cite{elyaniv2010selective,geifman2019selectivenet}.
Our empirical surface similarly screens service--delivery regimes using
evidence from other recordings. \new{Screening rejection reflects
insufficient historical support for accepting a setting. The separate
unobservable outcome means the reference or other evidence needed to
evaluate a contract is missing.}

\revision{This paper connects three decisions that arise after a twin
generates its estimates: whether the received state meets a service
contract, whether changing delivery restores a failed contract, and
whether a qualifying schedule exists at lower transmission cost.
Receiver reconstruction and a fixed source trajectory make those
comparisons directly testable. The optimistic qualification bound then
extends a failed delivery comparison into an infeasibility result over
a declared schedule class. Historical screening complements these
reference-based assessments by ranking settings for an unseen recording.
The surface, logistic model, and tree are alternative ways of using the
same service-contract evidence within this framework.}
\section{Service-Relative Fidelity Evaluation Framework}
\label{sec:methodology}

\revision{We consider a physical asset, a source twin, an IoT communication
path, and services at a remote receiver. Figure~\ref{fig:framework_overview}
shows how their recorded data enter the evaluation. Source-state
availability and packet delivery determine the state held by the receiver.
An offline evaluator compares that state with a recorded physical reference
under each service's requirements. The reference supplies evaluation
evidence and planning outcomes. Historical screening uses the resulting
labels from development recordings. Delivery interventions change arrival
histories while preserving the source estimates and reference.}

\begin{figure}[!tb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/framework_receiver_contract_overview.png}
    \caption{\newrev{Receiver-side service qualification. Physical-reference
    evidence from the asset, the twin state held after network delivery,
    and the service's accuracy, freshness, and evidence requirements enter
    the service-relative check. The outcome is qualified, failed
    (unqualified), or unobservable.}}
    \label{fig:framework_overview}
\end{figure}

Let the physical reference state at time $t$ be $\mathbf{x}_{P}(t)$ and the source-twin estimate generated with timestamp $\tau_i$ be $\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{T}(\tau_i)$. For the mobile robot experiments, the state may contain planar position $\mathbf{p}$, heading $\theta$, forward velocity $v$, and angular velocity $\omega$. Each service uses only the quantities required by its application. The physical reference is treated as a documented measurement system rather than error-free ground truth, and its supported quantities, uncertainty, timing, and valid intervals are reported for each dataset.

Throughout a delivery comparison, the source model and estimated trajectory are frozen; changing delivery changes which source states reach the receiver and when they arrive.

\subsection{Receiver-Held State and Discrepancy}

A delivery condition $d$ specifies the update rate, communication delay, jitter, and packet loss. Source state $i$ has timestamp $\tau_i$, becomes available after sensing and computation at time $b_i$, and arrives at the receiver at time $a_i^{(d)}$. A lost packet is represented by $a_i^{(d)}=\infty$, and causality requires
\begin{equation}
a_i^{(d)}\geq b_i.
\end{equation}

At receiver time $t$, the receiver selects the arrived packet containing the newest source timestamp:
\begin{equation}
i_d^{*}(t)
=
\underset{i:a_i^{(d)}\leq t}{\arg\max}\;\tau_i.
\end{equation}
The receiver-held state and its age are then
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}_{T}^{(d)}(t)
=
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{T}\!\left(\tau_{i_d^{*}(t)}\right),
\qquad
A_d(t)=t-\tau_{i_d^{*}(t)}.
\label{eq:receiver_state_age}
\end{equation}
Both quantities are unavailable if no packet has arrived. The receiver applies zero-order hold without extrapolation. Consequently, an older packet arriving late cannot replace a newer held state, and reading a cached state does not reset its age.

Source time and availability time are kept distinct. This is important when an estimator uses features, such as centered derivatives, that represent time $\tau_i$ but require later samples before becoming available. Any resulting computation or look-ahead delay must therefore be included in $b_i$ or evaluated through a separate causal implementation.

For a required quantity $q$, discrepancy under delivery condition $d$ is measured in its physical units:
\begin{equation}
D_q^{(d)}(t)
=
d_q\!\left(
x_{P,q}(t),
\widetilde{x}_{T,q}^{(d)}(t)
\right).
\end{equation}
For example, planar-position and heading discrepancies are
\begin{align}
D_p^{(d)}(t)
&=
\left\|
\mathbf{p}_{P}(t)
-
\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}_{T}^{(d)}(t)
\right\|_2,\\
D_{\theta}^{(d)}(t)
&=
\left|
\operatorname{wrap}\!\left(
\theta_{P}(t)
-
\widetilde{\theta}_{T}^{(d)}(t)
\right)
\right|.
\end{align}
\newrev{The operator $\operatorname{wrap}$ maps an angular difference to
$[-\pi,\pi)$, so heading discrepancy uses the shortest rotation.}

Global services use the discrepancy at the evaluation time. A local service with horizon $H_s$ instead evaluates relative motion over the completed interval $[t_k-H_s,t_k]$:
\begin{equation}
D_{q,H_s}^{(d)}(t_k)
=
d_q\!\left(
\Delta_{H_s}x_{P,q}(t_k),
\Delta_{H_s}\widetilde{x}_{T,q}^{(d)}(t_k)
\right),
\end{equation}
\rev{For position, $\Delta_{H_s}$ subtracts the interval endpoints and
rotates the displacement into that trajectory's starting heading. The
reference and receiver displacements are each expressed in their own
starting body frame before their Euclidean difference is taken. For
heading, the endpoint rotation and the difference between the two
rotations are wrapped to $[-\pi,\pi)$. This is the planar relative-pose
error used by the source-trajectory diagnostic as well. The local
contract checks freshness at the current endpoint and requires an
available cached state at the earlier endpoint.}
\newrev{The current endpoint determines how recently the evaluated
interval ends. The earlier endpoint supplies historical evidence for
the completed motion. Any mismatch from its cached state enters the
relative-motion discrepancy.}

\subsection{Service Qualification}

A service contract is defined as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{C}_s=
\left(
Q_s,H_s,\boldsymbol{\delta}_s,\Delta_s,
\eta_s,\gamma_s,\mathcal{O}_s
\right),
\end{equation}
where $Q_s$ is the set of required quantities, $H_s$ is the evaluation horizon, $\boldsymbol{\delta}_s$ contains the permitted physical discrepancies, and $\Delta_s$ is the maximum state age. The parameters $\eta_s\in(0,1]$ and $\gamma_s\in(0,1]$ specify the required satisfaction fraction and minimum observable fraction, respectively. The rule $\mathcal{O}_s$ specifies what information must be available to evaluate service \(s\).

Position, heading, and freshness limits remain in meters, degrees, and seconds, respectively. They are not combined through arbitrary weights into a single fidelity score.
At each evaluation time, a sample is observable if the reference,
receiver-held state, synchronization, history, and quality data required
by the service are available. A sample lacking this evidence lowers the
observable fraction but is excluded from the satisfaction fractions rather
than counted as a physical or freshness failure. For each observable
sample, the evaluator
checks whether the physical discrepancy is within every applicable
service tolerance and whether the receiver-held state is within the
service's maximum age. The sample passes jointly only when both checks
pass.

For a run, physical, freshness, and joint satisfaction
($S_s^P$, $S_s^F$, and $S_s^J$) are the fractions of observable samples
passing the physical check, freshness check, and both checks together,
respectively. The observable fraction, $\Omega_s$, is the fraction of
all evaluation samples for which the required evidence is available.
Satisfaction is undefined if no sample is observable. For a global
service, physical discrepancy is evaluated at the current time; for a
finite-horizon service, it is evaluated over the completed service
interval.
\rev{The query clock and its phase are part of the evaluation protocol.
Satisfaction measures compliance at those queries. Global pose has a
declared scoring window even though it has no finite local-motion
horizon.}
A run is qualified when at least $\gamma_s$ of its evaluation samples
are observable and at least $\eta_s$ of its observable samples pass
both the physical-error and freshness checks. It is unqualified when
the observability requirement is met but the joint pass fraction is
below $\eta_s$. If observability falls below $\gamma_s$, the result is
unobservable; it is not counted as either a pass or a failure.
We denote these outcomes by $q_s^{(d)}=1$, $0$, and $\varnothing$,
respectively, under delivery condition $d$.
\newrev{Qualification summarizes the declared evaluation window. Its
required pass fraction limits failures among observable queries.
Failure duration and clustering require a separate temporal summary.}

Because a sample passes jointly only when it passes both individual
checks, joint satisfaction cannot exceed physical or freshness
satisfaction. We report all three fractions to show which requirement
is associated with a failure, although qualification is determined by
the joint fraction and the observability requirement.

\subsection{Held-Out Qualification and Remediability}
\label{subsec:qualification_remediability}
To test transfer to an unseen recording, the historical surface scores
each service--delivery setting by the fraction of development recordings
that qualified under it. A cutoff selected from cross-fitted development
scores determines which settings to accept for the held-out recording.
Equal scores stay together, and the cutoff is frozen before testing.
Section~\ref{subsec:heldout_protocol} specifies the fitting and selection
procedure. The score expresses historical support for a setting, and
acceptance identifies a candidate for the service.

Performance is summarized by acceptance coverage and false-qualification risk:
\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{FQ}}
=
\frac{
\#\{\text{accepted held-out cases that fail}\}
}{
\#\{\text{accepted held-out cases}\}
}.
\end{equation}
Coverage and risk are reported together so each method's error rate can be interpreted alongside the fraction of cases it accepts. Statistical resampling uses physical sequences as the independent units, with timestamps and seeds retained within each sequence.

Finally, a remediability ledger determines whether delivery improvement can restore a failed service while the source twin remains fixed. Each observable sequence-service case is evaluated under degraded ($D$), practical ($P$), and ideal-available ($I$) delivery. The ideal condition forwards the full recorded source stream without added communication impairment.

Cases with $q_s^{(d)}=\varnothing$ under any tested delivery condition are excluded from the ledger and reported separately as unobservable. Among the remaining observable sequence-service pairs, categories are assigned in the following fixed precedence order so that each case receives exactly one label:
\begin{align}
\text{already qualified:}\quad
&q_s^{D}=1,\\
\text{delivery-remediable:}\quad
&q_s^{D}\neq 1,\quad q_s^{I}=1,\\
\text{persistent:}\quad
&q_s^{D}\neq 1,\quad q_s^{I}\neq 1.
\end{align}
The practical condition shows how many delivery-remediable cases are restored by the tested intermediate delivery setting.
\rev{Here, ideal-available describes delivery of the unimpeded full
stream. It is not an accuracy-maximizing selection of source states, so
qualification need not improve monotonically with update rate.}

\rev{For observable failures, physical-only means $S_s^P<\eta_s$ and
$S_s^F\geq\eta_s$, and freshness-only means the reverse. Both-marginal
failure means both are below $\eta_s$. Joint-only failure means both
marginals meet $\eta_s$ but $S_s^J$ does not, because their passing
samples overlap too little. These components describe which contract
checks fail. The paired ledger describes whether the tested delivery
change restores qualification.}
\subsection{\rev{Offline Delivery Feasibility and Planning}}
\label{subsec:offline_planning_method}

\rev{Offline planning uses the recorded reference to bound achievable
qualification and minimize transmissions. With combined state-availability
and network latency $L$, a candidate held state at query time $v$ has a
10-Hz source timestamp in $[v-1.5,v-L]$. The current endpoint must also
meet the service age limit. A local check includes a candidate at
$v=t_k-H_s$ whose timestamp cannot exceed the current state's timestamp.
The 1.5-s cap equals the largest study age limit and also bounds cached
historical states. Planning uses fully observable schedules on a common
set of $N_s$ queries, so all satisfaction denominators are fixed.}

\rev{Let $\mathcal{A}_{s,k}$ contain these admissible states or endpoint
pairs, and let $J_{s,k}^{(m)}(c)$ be one when choice $c$ passes both
checks for seed $m$ and zero otherwise. \newrev{The study uses three
repeated source-model fits per recording, indexed by $m$.} An optimistic upper bound is
\begin{equation}
U_s=\frac{1}{N_s}\sum_k
\max_{c\in\mathcal{A}_{s,k}}\frac{1}{3}\sum_{m=1}^{3}J_{s,k}^{(m)}(c).
\label{eq:optimistic_qualification_bound}
\end{equation}
An empty candidate set contributes zero. Any schedule in this class
selects an admissible choice at each query, whose pass fraction cannot
exceed that query's maximum. Averaging proves $S_s^J\leq U_s$.
Independent choices across queries make the bound optimistic, so
$U_s<\eta_s$ proves infeasibility, whereas $U_s\geq\eta_s$ requires a
consistent schedule to establish feasibility.}

\rev{For transmission minimization, let $r_b$ denote the update rate in
10-s receiver-time block $b$, selected from $\{1,2,5,10\}$~Hz, and let
\newrev{$N_{\mathrm{tx}}(\mathbf{r})$ count transmitted states under
schedule $\mathbf{r}$.} For the requested service set $\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{req}}$, the planner
solves
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\min_{\mathbf{r}}\quad & N_{\mathrm{tx}}(\mathbf{r})\\
\text{subject to}\quad & S_s^J(\mathbf{r})\geq\eta_s,
\quad s\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{req}},\\
& r_b\in\{1,2,5,10\}\text{~Hz for every block }b.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:offline_delivery_optimization}
\end{equation}
Here, $S_s^J$ is the mean satisfaction across three seeds under one
shared schedule. Blocks begin after the 30-s prefix. Every block starts
with an arrival, and its source timestamps are shifted back by $L$.
Consequently, the held state depends only on that receiver block's rate,
and a motion horizon of at most 10~s involves at most two adjacent blocks.
Precomputed pass counts for each rate pair form a mixed-integer flow
problem whose constraints select one consistent rate path. Costs count
updates arriving in the scored receiver window, including a partial
final block and excluding common prefix traffic. Every returned path is
checked by independent receiver replay.}

\rev{The optimization solver uses a zero relative-gap target and a 10-s limit per case.
Four time-limited feasible cases were refined with a 60-s limit.
Optimal status proves minimum cost within the block-rate family.
A feasible schedule at the time limit retains its reported cost gap.
Solver infeasibility rules out that family, while the bound in
\eqref{eq:optimistic_qualification_bound} covers the broader fully
observable bounded-age class.}
\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp_setup}

The experiments ask whether the state available to a receiver meets a
service's requirements, and whether changing delivery changes that outcome.
We keep each recorded source trajectory fixed, reconstruct the state held
by the receiver under each delivery condition, and compare it with the
physical reference using the service contract. This paired design isolates
delivery effects from changes to the source twin.

The mobile-robot setting allows controlled comparisons of recent motion
and current global pose using the same estimated position and heading.

\subsection{Data, Twin, and Service Contracts}

The primary study uses ten physical sequences from i2Nav
\cite{tang2025i2nav}: building00, building01, building02, parking00,
parking01, parking02, playground00, street00, street01, and street02.
The prepared records provide synchronized wheel/odometry and inertial
inputs, together with a physical-reference trajectory. The recordings span
the building, parking, playground, and street sequence categories indicated
by their names. Multiple physical sequences allow us to test transfer to a
recording excluded from the corresponding qualification fit.
\rev{The i2Nav recordings provide a physical reference trajectory together
with wheel-speed and inertial measurements for the twin. We align the
streams over their shared time interval, interpolate position and speed
to the 10-Hz evaluation rate, and unwrap heading before interpolation.
We convert the reference coordinates to the planar frame used to evaluate
the twin's position and heading. The dataset reports nominal reference
accuracy of 0.02~m in position and $0.01^\circ$ in attitude
\cite{i2navRobotDocumentation}. The prepared recordings span
1059.8--1871.3~s and have reference values throughout the scored
intervals.}

We generate the source trajectories with Twin V2, the sensor-based motion
estimator developed in this study. It corrects wheel/odometry forward
speed and IMU yaw rate, then propagates planar pose.
\rev{Its fast branch is a two-layer Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) with 64
hidden units and a 20-sample (2-s) sensor history. A two-layer, 32-unit
tanh branch summarizes 30~s of wheel--IMU consistency and motion to
estimate slow yaw correction. This correction remains zero until the
history is available. The accompanying reproducibility notes give the
six fast features, 16 slow features, and complete training settings.}
The twin predicts bounded corrections to odometer speed and IMU yaw rate:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
v_{T,k} &= v_{\mathrm{odo},k}+0.15\tanh(z_{v,k}),\\
\omega_{T,k} &= \omega_{\mathrm{imu},k}
 +0.020\tanh(z_{\omega,k})
 +0.005\tanh(z_{\mathrm{slow},k}).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:twin_bounded_corrections}
\end{equation}
The coefficients limit the speed correction to 0.15~m/s and the fast
and slow yaw corrections to 0.020 and 0.005~rad/s, respectively.
Using the corrected speed and yaw rate, the twin propagates planar
position and heading every 0.1~s by midpoint integration.
Training targets are reference-derived forward speed minus odometer speed
and reference-derived yaw rate minus IMU yaw rate. The slow target is the
training-only causal 30-s mean of the yaw correction; the fast yaw target
is the remaining correction. \rev{The frozen loss combines correction,
propagated-trajectory, and slow-bias objectives with the weights documented
in the reproducibility notes. Training uses AdamW with learning rate
$10^{-3}$, at most 25 epochs, early-stopping patience five, and batch size
eight.} Normalization is
fitted on training sequences, and validation sequences select checkpoints.
These settings define the source trajectories evaluated by the
service-relative framework.

All four services use the same position-and-heading stream from Twin V2,
but ask different questions of it. The local services assess motion over
1, 5, or 10 seconds, while global pose assesses current position and
heading. Keeping the twin and its inputs fixed makes this a controlled

contracts require physical, freshness, and joint satisfaction of at least
$\eta_s=0.80$, with an observable fraction of at least $\gamma_s=0.95$.
\newrev{The 1-s contract represents recent-motion monitoring. The 5-s and

Global pose supports location queries in a shared coordinate frame.
We set $\eta_s=0.80$ as the study's joint-satisfaction requirement,

\subsection{Receiver Evaluation and Remediability}

The primary replay crosses source-state selection rates of 1, 2, 5, and
10~Hz with added delays of 0, 25, 50, 100, and 200~ms. Rate reduction
selects existing states from the saved 10-Hz stream; it does not interpolate
or create states. At each 10-Hz evaluation time, the receiver holds the
arrived state with the newest source timestamp. Its age is the difference
between the evaluation time and that source timestamp. Re-reading a cached
state does not reset its age. Arrival and age comparisons use integer
nanoseconds. Since the source and evaluation clocks run at 10~Hz and
arrivals at an evaluation instant are included, 25-, 50-, and 100-ms
delays produce identical receiver-held histories in this replay.

\newrev{Centered derivative features require the next 10-Hz sensor
sample. We therefore distinguish three protocols. The source-time
benchmark assigns each precomputed centered state availability at its
source timestamp. Buffered-centered replay uses exactly the same states
but adds 100~ms before network delivery. The past-only bank instead
recomputes features with backward differences and retrains the source
models, assigning no additional computation delay. In buffered replay,
arrival time equals source timestamp plus 100~ms plus the configured
network delay. The source timestamp used to calculate age is unchanged.
Table~\ref{tab:timing_protocols} specifies availability for the nested banks
and the supporting ordinary-LOSO comparison. The source-time centered
protocol is a precomputed benchmark. Buffering accounts for its one-sample
look-ahead. Planning uses buffered-centered and past-only estimates.}

\subsection{Held-Out Qualification Protocol and Comparators}
\label{subsec:heldout_protocol}

This analysis tests whether qualification results from earlier recordings can help an operator choose service--delivery settings to consider for a
new recording. A setting is one service paired with one update rate and delay. The service--delivery surface scores each setting by the fraction of development recordings that qualified under its contract.

We use ten physical sequences in a doubly nested leave-one-sequence-out
evaluation. In each outer fold, one sequence is held out for testing and
the other nine are used for development. The
recordings share the same robot, sensors, and state-estimation
pipeline, while covering several environments and showing
different error patterns. This gives the qualification rule a
common basis for learning from nine recordings, while leaving
meaningful variation for testing on the tenth. LOSO therefore
measures transfer to an unseen recording under the same study
setup. Each sequence has 80
service--delivery cells: four services crossed with 20 rate--delay
settings. Across the ten folds, this produces 800 held-out cells.
Because cells from the same sequence are related, the physical sequence,
not each individual cell, is the unit used for uncertainty estimates.

Table~\ref{tab:exp_comparators} compares the surface with six alternatives. Each uses a different
amount or form of information to rank service--delivery settings. For
the label-based methods, qualification labels come from the service
contracts defined earlier. The AoI-only method ranks settings using age
rather than those labels.

The six motion features use only the first 30~s of sensor data: mean and
95th-percentile absolute forward speed, mean and 95th-percentile
absolute IMU yaw rate, 95th-percentile absolute forward acceleration,
and the fraction of samples with absolute IMU yaw rate above
0.1~rad/s. No physical-reference error is used as a predictor.

For each outer test sequence, we choose a cutoff using only the other
nine sequences. We first take turns holding out each development
sequence. Its score is computed using a rule fitted to the other eight,
\rev{so its own labels are excluded from the screening fit.} We combine the cross-fitted cell
scores from all nine development recordings and choose the cutoff whose acceptance is closest to
the requested target. Equal scores are kept together rather than split;
if two cutoffs are equally close, we choose the higher one. We then fit
the method using all nine development sequences and apply the selected
cutoff unchanged to the outer test sequence.
\revision{For the buffered-centered experiment, we replay all 300 saved
trajectories with the added availability delay, recompute seed-averaged
qualification labels, and repeat every screening fit and cutoff selection.
The source estimates, service thresholds, sensor-prefix features, and
nominal rate--delay grid remain fixed. This paired protocol isolates the
effect of state availability across both development and test recordings.}
\rev{This inner cross-fit refits the screen over fixed development
trajectories. The source pipeline for development recording $j$ excludes
the outer test and $j$, while other development recordings may contribute
to that pipeline. The outer test remains excluded from every source fit,
screening fit, and cutoff-selection step.}

For example, when \texttt{building00} is the test sequence, each of the
other nine recordings is scored using a rule fitted to the remaining
eight. The combined cell scores from those nine recordings determine the
cutoff. The rule is then fitted
using all nine development recordings and applied to
\texttt{building00}. The test sequence does not affect the cutoff or
fitting. Its accepted fraction can differ from the requested target
because its scores may differ from the development scores and tied
scores cannot be split.

The target guides cutoff selection on development data and the accepted
test count follows from applying that frozen threshold. For example,
at the 25\% request, AoI-only accepts 160 of 800 \newrev{source-time centered} cells
and motion logistic accepts 199. The
per-service counts use the same pooled cutoff; each service does not
receive its own separately selected cutoff.

For each method, false-qualification risk is the number of accepted
cells that fail the contract divided by the total number of accepted
cells. We report risk with the actual accepted count and coverage. If
no cells are accepted, risk is undefined. The held-out labels are used
only to evaluate the accepted cells, not to choose the cutoff. The
target range from 10\% to 50\% shows how risk changes as the screening
rule accepts more or fewer settings.
\rev{The target controls acceptance rather than prescribing an error
rate. Reported risks give equal weight to the tested service--delivery
cells. Intervals use 5,000 resamples of ten complete recordings with
their frozen held-out predictions and cutoffs. The 2.5th and 97.5th
percentiles of finite risk values give the endpoints, with resamples
accepting no cases omitted. \newrev{This estimates variation across
recordings for the already fitted source and screening models.}}

The resulting surface is an offline screening aid: it shows which
tested service--delivery settings have support in prior recordings,
so an operator can decide which settings to investigate or provision.
\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We begin with the difference between local motion and global pose, then
follow one recording through the receiver-side evaluation. We next
examine qualification across the delivery grid and identify which
failures improve when delivery changes. \new{We then test whether
delivery can be scheduled to support several services with fewer
transmissions. Component comparisons and measured packet arrivals follow.
The final results examine held-out qualification, contract sensitivity,
feature timing, and applications to UGV01 and MAGNET.}

\subsection{Local and Global Fidelity Can Give Different Assessments}
\label{subsec:local_global_result}

Before considering network delivery, we examine the source trajectories themselves.
Figure~\ref{fig:local_global} compares two ways of describing their error. Each
point represents one of ten physical sequences, with the three model seeds averaged
within that sequence. The horizontal axis is 10-s translational relative-pose RMSE
($RPE_{10}$), which describes error in motion over a finite interval. The vertical
axis is the 95th-percentile global position discrepancy ($D_p$ p95), which describes
how far the reported position is from the physical reference.
\rev{The RMSE summarizes relative-motion error, while the global p95
shows the tail of position discrepancy. Their different summaries motivate
separate service checks rather than define qualification thresholds.}

The two measures rank the recordings differently. For example,
parking02 has a low $RPE_{10}$ of 0.097~m but a $D_p$ p95 of 22.345~m. Parking00
has a higher $RPE_{10}$ of 0.453~m but a lower $D_p$ p95 of 3.086~m. In other
words, a trajectory can represent recent motion well even when its
reported global position is far from the reference. A single trajectory
error measure would miss this difference, which is why the service
contracts test local motion and global pose separately.

\subsection{Worked Example: Applying the Framework to One Recording}
\label{subsec:worked_result}

\newrev{The source-time centered benchmark on held-out
\texttt{building00} shows how the framework turns received states into
service decisions. We keep its recorded twin trajectory fixed and change
delivery, so the receiver holds different states from the same stream.}

The example applies two service contracts to the same recording.
The local-1-s service evaluates whether the twin reproduces the
robot's motion over the preceding one second, using position and
heading tolerances of 0.10~m and $2^\circ$. The global-pose service
evaluates the robot's current position and heading, with tolerances
of 1.0~m and $5^\circ$. Their maximum permitted state ages are
0.6~s and 1.0~s, respectively. For both services, at least 80\% of
observable samples must meet the physical, freshness, and joint
requirements, and at least 95\% of evaluation samples must be
observable.

At each evaluation time, the receiver holds the newest state that has
arrived. The framework compares that state with the physical reference
and checks its age against the service limit. Joint satisfaction is the
fraction of observable samples that pass both checks. Coverage is the
fraction of evaluation samples with enough evidence to make those checks.

Figure~\ref{fig:worked_result} compares degraded delivery
(2~Hz/200~ms), practical delivery (5~Hz/50~ms), and
ideal-available delivery (10~Hz/0~ms). All samples are observable
(coverage is 1.000), and freshness satisfaction is 1.000 for both
services under all three conditions. At the declared 10-Hz evaluation
instants, the maximum age under 2-Hz/200-ms delivery is 0.6~s,
which meets the inclusive local-1-s age limit. Between those instants,
age can approach 0.7~s. The ledger uses this sampled-time evaluation.
Consequently, in this example,
joint satisfaction equals physical satisfaction. The distinction
between the services comes from the physical discrepancy of the
receiver-held states.

For local-1-s motion, joint satisfaction is 0.675 under degraded
delivery, 0.874 under practical delivery, and 0.958 under
ideal-available delivery. The required value is 0.80, so this service
fails under degraded delivery and passes under both practical and
ideal-available delivery. This is a delivery-remediable case, and
the practical condition restores qualification.

Global pose gives a different result on the same recording. Its joint
satisfaction is 0.165 under degraded delivery, 0.049 under practical
delivery, and 0.008 under ideal-available delivery. None reaches
0.80, so the global-pose service remains unqualified under all three
tested conditions, including ideal-available delivery. The local
service checks motion over one second, while the global service checks
position in the overall coordinate frame. More frequent states can
improve the motion comparison without correcting the source
trajectory's global position. On this recording, the states selected
under practical and ideal-available delivery yield lower global-pose
satisfaction than those selected under degraded delivery.

The reported values average three model-seed results for this one
physical recording.

\subsection{Contribution of the Evaluation Components}
\label{subsec:component_comparison}

Figure~\ref{fig:component_comparison} shows how qualification decisions
change when individual checks are removed. In the primary grid,
source accuracy alone passes
680 of 800 cases, while receiver-held physical accuracy passes 390.
The 290 cases that change status show why judging the source trajectory
alone can approve settings that fail after delivery.
\rev{The two-way audit finds 390 common passes, 290 source-only passes,
120 common failures, and no receiver-only passes on this grid.}

Under the original age limits, the receiver-physical, separate-check, and
complete-contract decisions coincide. To expose the contribution of freshness,
the right panel uses the saved ordinary-LOSO study with a common 0.2-s age
limit under 5-Hz delivery, 50-ms delay, 20-ms jitter standard deviation,
and 1\% configured packet loss. Of its 40 sequence--service cases, source
accuracy passes 34 and receiver physical accuracy passes 31. Requiring
freshness as well reduces the count to 12. Requiring joint satisfaction
reduces it to eight.

The final four exclusions are local-1-s and local-5-s motion on
\texttt{street01} and \texttt{street02}. Their physical and freshness
satisfaction fractions each exceed 0.80, but their joint fractions range
from 0.754 to 0.779. Each requirement is met often enough individually,
yet they are not met together often enough to qualify. The comparison
therefore identifies the decisions contributed by receiver reconstruction,
freshness, and their temporal overlap.
\revision{The reference-masking check exercises the remaining evidence
requirement. On \texttt{building00} local-1-s motion, masking
$[60,72)$~s leaves 11,569 of 11,699 queries observable
($\Omega_s=0.989$), and the case remains qualified. Masking
$[60,180)$~s leaves 10,489 observable queries ($\Omega_s=0.897$).
The remaining samples still achieve mean joint satisfaction of 0.954,
but coverage is below 0.95, so the outcome is unobservable. The
framework therefore distinguishes adequate performance on available

\label{subsec:heldout_results}

The ledger describes outcomes on recordings whose service decisions are
known. We next ask whether results from earlier recordings can guide the
choice of service and delivery settings for a new recording. In the
doubly nested leave-one-sequence-out analysis, each outer test recording
is excluded from both qualification fitting and cutoff selection.

\revision{Table~\ref{tab:heldout_risk} compares all three timing protocols

Buffering the same estimates by 100~ms changes those counts to 33 false
qualifications among 156 accepted settings, or 21.2\% risk at 19.5\%
acceptance. With separately trained past-only features, the corresponding
result is 19/168, or 11.3\% risk at 21.0\% acceptance. Each protocol uses
its own development-selected cutoffs and is evaluated on the same ten
outer test recordings.}

\revision{The buffered comparison changes only availability before

recording-bootstrap interval of 6.2--31.4 points. Thus, the low risk of the
source-time benchmark does not persist when centered-feature buffering
is included. The past-only experiment tests a different remedy: changing
the features and retraining the estimator to produce a new source stream.}

\subsection{Sensitivity to Contract Requirements and Freshness}
\label{subsec:sensitivity_results}

\rev{The ledger reports outcomes for declared service requirements, so
changing those requirements can move a case between categories.}
Figure~\ref{fig:threshold_sensitivity_result} varies one requirement at
a time across the same 40 recording--service cases. The left panel
scales each service's position and heading tolerances. The middle panel
changes the fraction of observable samples that must pass both the
physical-error and freshness checks. The right panel scales each
service's maximum permitted state age. All other requirements remain
at their baseline values.

At the baseline satisfaction requirement of 0.80, the ledger contains
30 delivery-remediable, six persistent, and four already-qualified
cases. At a requirement of 0.70, the counts are 17, five, and 18.
At 0.90, they are 29, nine, and two. \rev{Tighter requirements can move
an already-qualified case into the remediable group, or move a remediable
case into the persistent group. The remediable count can therefore rise
or fall as a threshold changes.}

\subsection{\rev{Feature Timing and Query-Clock Sensitivity}}
\label{subsec:feature_timing_results}

\newrev{Under the 100-ms centered buffer defined in
Table~\ref{tab:timing_protocols}, the ledger has 31 remediable,
six persistent, and three already-qualified cases. Practical delivery
restores 17 of the 31 remediable cases. The labels remain close to the
source-time ledger, but the intermediate delivery point restores fewer
services because availability delay consumes part of their error and age
budgets. Section~\ref{subsec:heldout_results} evaluates this same buffer
through the complete nested screening protocol. Ordinary-LOSO past-only
retraining provides an additional source-estimator comparison: its ledger
contains 28 remediable, eight persistent, and four already-qualified cases.
Practical delivery restores 26 of 28, with mean ATE changing from 2.398 to 3.882~m
and mean 1-s RPE from 0.061 to 0.150~m.}

\rev{We also shift the three ledger conditions' 10-Hz query clocks by
25, 50, and 75~ms. Receiver states remain held exactly as delivered,
while reference position and unwrapped heading are interpolated to the
shifted queries. Relative to the aligned clock, at most one of 40 ledger
labels changes in either bank. Practical restoration ranges from 27 to
29 cases with centered features and from 24 to 29 with past-only
features. Exact integration between arrivals gives approximately 0.80
time-weighted freshness for the local-1-s service under 2-Hz/200-ms
delivery, compared with 1.000 at aligned queries. Query timing therefore
affects satisfaction even when most ledger labels remain stable.}

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\revision{This work connects service-specific fidelity measurement to
delivery decisions. Receiver reconstruction exposes failures that source
accuracy and age compliance alone miss. Paired replay then identifies
which failed services recover when their arrival history changes. The
offline qualification bound can rule out recovery within the declared
bounded-age schedule class, while rate-block optimization quantifies the
transmissions needed when qualifying schedules exist. These results give
an operator a concrete sequence of decisions: specify the service and
available evidence, assess qualification, test delivery recovery, and
compare communication costs.}

\revision{Historical screening extends recorded evidence to candidate
settings on an unseen recording. Its performance depends on the source
stream and its availability, as shown by the controlled 100-ms buffered
comparison and the separately trained past-only bank. Screening evidence
should therefore use the timing configuration intended for operation.
The resulting recorded-workload benchmarks provide a basis for testing
online policies that estimate service adequacy and choose updates from
information available at decision time.}

Analysis code and derived evaluation artifacts are available at
\url{https://github.com/CEISCA-VT/DigitalTwinDivergence}. The source
datasets are available from their cited providers.
\rev{Accompanying reproducibility notes provide the complete planning
formulation, source-model settings, and service/recording screening
breakdowns. The review audit records input hashes and reproduction checks.}

Future work will evaluate additional physical systems and network paths
with end-to-end state-availability timing, and \new{develop online
scheduling from evidence available at decision time.}
