\title{Digital Twin Fidelity for Networked IoT Services}
\author{Shreyas Udaya, Aiden Wiehn, Sreya Viswanadha, Leyla Nazhandali}
\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Digital twins provide virtual representations of physical assets for
monitoring and decision support in Internet of Things systems. Their
practicality depends on whether the information available to a service
meets its accuracy and freshness requirements, not only on the quality
of estimates at their source. We present a service-relative fidelity
framework that evaluates the state held by a receiver against declared
physical-error limits, time horizons, age bounds, and evidence requirements.
It reports qualified, unqualified, or unobservable outcomes. A paired
delivery-remediability test holds the source estimates fixed and changes
their delivery to determine whether improved communication restores a
failed service. A complementary held-out procedure uses prior recordings
to screen service--delivery regimes. Across ten i2Nav sequences, practical
delivery restored 27 of 30 cases classified as delivery-remediable. At a
25\% requested acceptance, the held-out surface produced four false
qualifications among 200 accepted cases (2.0\%) with precomputed centered features;
a separately trained past-only backward-difference bank produced 19 among 168
(11.3\%). These results show why source accuracy and age compliance alone
do not establish receiver-side adequacy, and how a paired delivery test
can identify when changing delivery restores a service outcome. Evaluation
also includes measured Linux network-emulation traces, component comparisons,
and supporting robot and thermal-twin studies.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT) systems connect physical assets to edge and cloud services that monitor their operation and support decisions. In these systems, a digital twin is useful only when the information available to a consuming service is accurate and recent enough for its task. This requirement is increasingly relevant as robots and other connected machines become more capable and widely deployed. The International Federation of Robotics reports five million industrial robots operating in factories in 2025 and growing investment in virtual environments for robotics \cite{ifr2026robots}, illustrating the expanding connected-automation setting in which reliable physical-to-digital information matters.

A robot digital twin produces estimates of the physical robot's state, such as position, heading, or speed. In an IIoT deployment, these estimates may be sent from an on-robot or edge-side twin to a service running on an edge server, cloud platform, or another connected system. We call the destination that receives and uses the estimates the \emph{receiver}, and the consuming application the \emph{service}. The receiver can use only information that has arrived, which may be older than the twin's latest estimate.

Whether a received state is useful depends on the service. A service that evaluates recent motion may tolerate some accumulated error in absolute position, while a service that needs the robot's current location may not. Conversely, a state may be accurate for the time it represents but too old to support a time-sensitive decision. A single trajectory-accuracy value therefore cannot, by itself, establish whether the information at the receiver is suitable for every service.

This distinction matters when diagnosing a service failure. If the source estimates meet the service's physical-error requirements but arrive too late, improved delivery may help. If the service still fails under the best tested delivery, delivery changes alone have not restored it. Missing reference measurements, poor synchronization, or inadequate data quality can also make the outcome unobservable. Keeping these outcomes separate shows what the evidence supports and which intervention is relevant.

Prior work has developed methods for measuring digital-twin fidelity,
trajectory error, and information freshness. The question addressed here
is whether the state that has reached a receiver is sufficiently accurate
and recent for the service that will use it. Mu{\~n}oz et al. use trace
alignment to assess physical–digital correspondence \cite{munoz2024trace};
Yates et al. survey Age of Information in networked systems
\cite{yates2021aoi}.

We address this question with a service-relative evaluation framework. Each service declares the state quantities it needs, the time horizon over which they are evaluated, acceptable physical discrepancy, a maximum state age, and the evidence required for evaluation. The framework reports whether observable cases meet both the physical and freshness requirements, and marks cases with insufficient evidence as \emph{unobservable}. It also builds a qualification surface from prior physical sequences to screen service--delivery regimes for consideration; the surface is an offline guide, not a per-state accuracy check.

We then use a paired delivery-remediability analysis. For each recorded source trajectory, we keep the twin estimates fixed and evaluate receiver-held states under degraded, practical, and ideal-available delivery. A case is delivery-remediable when it fails under degraded delivery but qualifies under ideal-available delivery; a case that fails under both is persistent under this comparison. Cases that pass even under degraded delivery are already qualified. Because the source trajectory is fixed, differences between these outcomes reflect the tested delivery conditions.

We evaluate the framework primarily on ten physical sequences from the i2Nav dataset \cite{tang2025i2nav}.
At the 25\% requested-acceptance operating point, the surface produced four false qualifications among 200 accepted cases (2.0\%) with precomputed centered features and 19 among 168 (11.3\%) with separately trained past-only features. The centered-feature surface accepted no global-service cases at this point. In the paired delivery analysis, practical saved-state replay restored 27 of the 30 delivery-remediable cases. We also replay measured UDP packet arrivals from a Linux virtual network interface shaped with \texttt{tc netem}, and apply the ledger to position-reference data from UGV01.
The contributions of this work are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
    \setlength{\parsep}{0pt}
    \setlength{\topsep}{2pt}
    \item We develop a service-relative digital twin fidelity framework that evaluates the state available to a network receiver against a service's physical-error, freshness, and evidence requirements. The framework distinguishes qualified, unqualified, and unobservable outcomes without collapsing position error, heading error, and state age into a single score.

    \item We introduce a paired delivery-remediability analysis that holds the source twin and its recorded estimates fixed while changing delivery conditions. This identifies whether improved delivery restores a failed service or whether it remains unqualified under the tested ideal-available delivery.

    \item We evaluate service qualification across held-out physical sequences and demonstrate the framework with sampled delivery, measured Linux \texttt{tc netem} packet traces, and a position-only UGV01 study.
\end{enumerate}
\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

Digital-twin evaluation builds on verification, validation, and
model-credibility research. These approaches assess a digital representation
against its physical counterpart and the use it is intended to support
\cite{jones2020characterising,shao2023credibility,bitencourt2025vv}.
This matters because aggregate resemblance alone does not establish that
a twin's reported state is adequate for the decisions made by consuming
applications.

Prior research has developed methods for comparing physical and virtual
behavior. Su \emph{et al.} characterize digital-twin identicality using
properties including trueness, precision, completeness, and latency
\cite{su2023identicality}. Mu{\~n}oz \emph{et al.} use trace alignment to
compare physical and digital executions \cite{munoz2024trace}. For mobile robots,
absolute and relative pose error metrics summarize global trajectory
error and local motion error, respectively \cite{sturm2012rgbd}. These
approaches provide important ways to assess model behavior. Our work asks
a related but distinct receiver-side question: does the state that has
actually arrived satisfy the requirements of the specified service?

This question also connects digital twin evaluation to information
freshness in networked systems. Age of Information (AoI) measures how
long it has been since the freshest available update was generated
\cite{kaul2012realtime,yates2021aoi}. Extensions consider the
application level usefulness of information or its freshness when a
query is made \cite{maatouk2023aoii,chiariotti2022query}. Research on
digital twin synchronization similarly studies how update and
communication decisions affect the relationship between physical and
virtual states \cite{tan2024synchronization,li2025budget}. Together,
these studies establish that delivery rate, delay, and update timing
affect the value of a networked twin. The receiver's information,
however, can be fresh but physically inaccurate, or physically accurate
at generation but too old when used.

Selective prediction research provides another useful foundation: a
system should withhold a decision when the available evidence is
insufficient \cite{elyaniv2010selective,geifman2019selectivenet}.
Accordingly, we report qualification with coverage and mark missing
reference or synchronization evidence as unobservable. In this
work, the empirical qualification surface estimates whether a
service-delivery regime is supported on a held-out physical sequence
using evidence from other sequences, providing an offline basis for comparing candidate delivery regimes for a service.

Building on these foundations, this paper connects receiver-side service
qualification with a controlled assessment of delivery remediability.
The evaluator reconstructs the state available to a service, checks its
physical discrepancy and freshness against declared requirements, and
retains an unobservable outcome when required evidence is missing.
Holding the generated source trajectory fixed across delivery histories
then identifies which failed cases qualify when delivery improves.
A held-out analysis tests whether evidence from prior recordings can
screen service--delivery settings for an unseen recording. The contribution
is this combined evaluation procedure and its experimental evidence,
with the historical surface providing an interpretable screening rule.
\section{Service-Relative Fidelity Evaluation Framework}
\label{sec:methodology}

We consider a mobile robot, a digital twin that estimates its state, an IoT communication path, and services that consume the state available at a remote receiver. The methodology distinguishes the state generated by the source twin from the state actually held by the receiver. This distinction allows physical-model error, communication-induced degradation, and insufficient evidence to be evaluated separately.

Let the physical reference state at time $t$ be $\mathbf{x}_{P}(t)$ and the source-twin estimate generated with timestamp $\tau_i$ be $\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{T}(\tau_i)$. For the mobile robot experiments, the state may contain planar position $\mathbf{p}$, heading $\theta$, forward velocity $v$, and angular velocity $\omega$. Each service uses only the quantities required by its application. The physical reference is treated as a documented measurement system rather than error-free ground truth, and its supported quantities, uncertainty, timing, and valid intervals are reported for each dataset.

Throughout a delivery comparison, the source model and estimated trajectory are frozen; changing delivery changes which source states reach the receiver and when they arrive.

\subsection{Receiver-Held State and Discrepancy}

A delivery condition $d$ specifies the update rate, communication delay, jitter, and packet loss. Source state $i$ has timestamp $\tau_i$, becomes available after sensing and computation at time $b_i$, and arrives at the receiver at time $a_i^{(d)}$. A lost packet is represented by $a_i^{(d)}=\infty$, and causality requires
\begin{equation}
a_i^{(d)}\geq b_i.
\end{equation}

At receiver time $t$, the receiver selects the arrived packet containing the newest source timestamp:
\begin{equation}
i_d^{*}(t)
=
\underset{i:a_i^{(d)}\leq t}{\arg\max}\;\tau_i.
\end{equation}
The receiver-held state and its age are then
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}_{T}^{(d)}(t)
=
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{T}\!\left(\tau_{i_d^{*}(t)}\right),
\qquad
A_d(t)=t-\tau_{i_d^{*}(t)}.
\label{eq:receiver_state_age}
\end{equation}
Both quantities are unavailable if no packet has arrived. The receiver applies zero-order hold without extrapolation. Consequently, an older packet arriving late cannot replace a newer held state, and reading a cached state does not reset its age.

Source time and availability time are kept distinct. This is important when an estimator uses features, such as centered derivatives, that represent time $\tau_i$ but require later samples before becoming available. Any resulting computation or look-ahead delay must therefore be included in $b_i$ or evaluated through a separate causal implementation.

For a required quantity $q$, discrepancy under delivery condition $d$ is measured in its physical units:
\begin{equation}
D_q^{(d)}(t)
=
d_q\!\left(
x_{P,q}(t),
\widetilde{x}_{T,q}^{(d)}(t)
\right).
\end{equation}
For example, planar-position and heading discrepancies are
\begin{align}
D_p^{(d)}(t)
&=
\left\|
\mathbf{p}_{P}(t)
-
\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}_{T}^{(d)}(t)
\right\|_2,\\
D_{\theta}^{(d)}(t)
&=
\left|
\operatorname{wrap}\!\left(
\theta_{P}(t)
-
\widetilde{\theta}_{T}^{(d)}(t)
\right)
\right|.
\end{align}

Global services use the discrepancy at the evaluation time. A local service with horizon $H_s$ instead evaluates relative motion over the completed interval $[t_k-H_s,t_k]$:
\begin{equation}
D_{q,H_s}^{(d)}(t_k)
=
d_q\!\left(
\Delta_{H_s}x_{P,q}(t_k),
\Delta_{H_s}\widetilde{x}_{T,q}^{(d)}(t_k)
\right),
\end{equation}
where $\Delta_{H_s}$ denotes the change between the two interval endpoints. Thus, both global and local services are evaluated using the states actually available to the receiver.

\subsection{Service Qualification}

A service contract is defined as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{C}_s=
\left(
Q_s,H_s,\boldsymbol{\delta}_s,\Delta_s,
\eta_s,\gamma_s,\mathcal{O}_s
\right),
\end{equation}
where $Q_s$ is the set of required quantities, $H_s$ is the evaluation horizon, $\boldsymbol{\delta}_s$ contains the permitted physical discrepancies, and $\Delta_s$ is the maximum state age. The parameters $\eta_s\in(0,1]$ and $\gamma_s\in(0,1]$ specify the required satisfaction fraction and minimum observable fraction, respectively. The rule $\mathcal{O}_s$ defines the evidence needed to evaluate the contract.

Position, heading, and freshness limits remain in meters, angular units, and seconds, respectively. They are not combined through arbitrary weights into a single fidelity score.
At each evaluation time, a sample is observable if the reference,
receiver-held state, synchronization, history, and quality data required
by the service are available. A sample lacking this evidence lowers the
observable fraction but is excluded from the satisfaction fractions rather
than counted as a physical or freshness failure. For each observable
sample, the evaluator
checks whether the physical discrepancy is within every applicable
service tolerance and whether the receiver-held state is within the
service's maximum age. The sample passes jointly only when both checks
pass.

For a run, physical, freshness, and joint satisfaction
($S_s^P$, $S_s^F$, and $S_s^J$) are the fractions of observable samples
passing the physical check, freshness check, and both checks together,
respectively. The observable fraction, $\Omega_s$, is the fraction of
all evaluation samples for which the required evidence is available.
Satisfaction is undefined if no sample is observable. For a global
service, physical discrepancy is evaluated at the current time; for a
finite-horizon service, it is evaluated over the completed service
interval.
A run is qualified when at least $\gamma_s$ of its evaluation samples
are observable and at least $\eta_s$ of its observable samples pass
both the physical-error and freshness checks. It is unqualified when
the observability requirement is met but the joint pass fraction is
below $\eta_s$. If observability falls below $\gamma_s$, the result is
unobservable; it is not counted as either a pass or a failure.

Because a sample passes jointly only when it passes both individual
checks, joint satisfaction cannot exceed physical or freshness
satisfaction. We report all three fractions to show which requirement
is associated with a failure, although qualification is determined by
the joint fraction and the observability requirement.

\subsection{Held-Out Qualification and Remediability}
To test whether qualification transfers to an unseen physical sequence, we
use the other nine sequences to score each service--delivery setting. The
score is the fraction of those sequences that qualified under that
setting. We select the acceptance cutoff using cross-fitted scores from
the nine development sequences: each sequence is scored using the other
eight, so it does not score itself. We choose the cutoff whose
development-set acceptance is closest to the target, keeping cases with
equal scores together. The selected cutoff is then frozen and applied
to the held-out sequence.

We apply this threshold unchanged to the held-out sequence, so its achieved acceptance need not equal the target. For example, at a 25 \% target, AoI-only accepts 160/800 held-out cells (20 \%), while motion logistic accepts 199/800 (24.9 \%). Their different score distributions and ties explain the different counts. The score is an empirical transfer statistic, not a calibrated probability of safety.

Performance is summarized by acceptance coverage and false-qualification risk:
\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{FQ}}
=
\frac{
\#\{\text{accepted held-out cases that fail}\}
}{
\#\{\text{accepted held-out cases}\}
}.
\end{equation}
Coverage and risk are reported together so each method's error rate can be interpreted alongside the fraction of cases it accepts. Statistical resampling uses physical sequences as the independent units, with timestamps and seeds retained within each sequence.

Finally, a remediability ledger determines whether delivery improvement can restore a failed service while the source twin remains fixed. Each observable sequence-service case is evaluated under degraded ($D$), practical ($P$), and ideal-available ($I$) delivery. The ideal condition forwards the full recorded source stream without added communication impairment.

Cases with $q_s^{(d)}=\varnothing$ under any tested delivery condition are excluded from the ledger and reported separately as unobservable. Among the remaining observable sequence-service pairs, categories are assigned in the following fixed precedence order so that each case receives exactly one label:
\begin{align}
\text{already qualified:}\quad
&q_s^{D}=1,\\
\text{delivery-remediable:}\quad
&q_s^{D}\neq 1,\quad q_s^{I}=1,\\
\text{persistent:}\quad
&q_s^{D}\neq 1,\quad q_s^{I}\neq 1
\end{align}.
The practical condition shows how many delivery-remediable cases are restored by the tested intermediate delivery setting.

For interpretation, failed cases are additionally labeled as physical-only, freshness-only, both-marginal, or joint-only failures using $S_s^{P}$, $S_s^{F}$, and $S_s^{J}$. Failure components are defined from the run-level satisfaction fractions,
using the contract threshold $\eta_s$. A \emph{both-marginal failure}
occurs when $S_s^P<\eta_s$ and $S_s^F<\eta_s$. A
\emph{joint-only failure} occurs when both marginal requirements pass,
$S_s^P\geq\eta_s$ and $S_s^F\geq\eta_s$, but the joint requirement
fails, $S_s^J<\eta_s$. The latter can occur because the samples that
satisfy the physical requirement are not necessarily the same samples
that satisfy the freshness requirement. Delivery-remediable cases show that improved delivery lets the receiver use states in the frozen source stream to meet the contract; persistent cases remain unqualified under the tested ideal-available condition.
\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp_setup}

The experiments ask whether the state available to a receiver meets a
service's requirements, and whether changing delivery changes that outcome.
We keep each recorded source trajectory fixed, reconstruct the state held
by the receiver under each delivery condition, and compare it with the
physical reference using the service contract. This paired design isolates
delivery effects from changes to the source twin.

The robot setting makes this distinction visible. Position and heading can
support services with different time needs: one service may assess recent
motion, while another needs a reliable current pose. Errors in speed and
turning can also accumulate over time, so a trajectory that represents
short-term motion well may still have substantial global position error.
The study uses this setting to test whether service-specific evaluation
reveals differences that one trajectory-wide score would hide.

\subsection{Data, Twin, and Service Contracts}

The primary study uses ten physical sequences from i2Nav
\cite{tang2025i2nav}: building00, building01, building02, parking00,
parking01, parking02, playground00, street00, street01, and street02.
The prepared records provide synchronized wheel/odometry and inertial
inputs, together with a physical-reference trajectory. The recordings span
the building, parking, playground, and street sequence categories indicated
by their names. Multiple physical sequences allow us to test transfer to a
recording excluded from the corresponding qualification fit.

The reference has distinct roles. It provides supervised targets during
training, initializes the first pose, and supports retrospective fidelity
evaluation. It is not an input to the learned V2 predictor. In the evaluated
EKF replay, quality-gated GNSS measurements are used as filter updates;
the state is not repeatedly reset to ground truth. Thus, GNSS is not a
learned-model feature, but it can affect the propagated EKF trajectory.
Reference and twin are compared on a common 10-Hz clock in a local ENU-style
planar frame, without post-hoc spatial registration. Heading is unwrapped
for interpolation and wrapped when angular differences are calculated.

The source model is Twin V2, a sensor-based motion estimator. It corrects
wheel/odometry forward speed and IMU yaw rate, then propagates planar pose.
The fast branch is a two-layer Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) with 64 hidden
units, 0.10 inter-layer dropout, and a 20-sample (2-s) history. Its six
features are odometer speed, IMU yaw rate, odometer acceleration, IMU yaw
acceleration, absolute yaw rate, and absolute odometer acceleration. The
slow branch is a two-layer, 32-unit tanh network using 16 rolling statistics
over the previous 300 samples (30~s): mean, standard deviation, RMS, and
mean magnitude of IMU yaw and wheel yaw; mean, standard deviation, and RMS
of wheel--IMU yaw disagreement; mean and standard deviation of normalized
disagreement; and mean, standard deviation, and mean magnitude of forward
speed. The slow yaw correction is held at zero until its full history is
available.
The twin predicts bounded corrections to odometer speed and IMU yaw rate:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
v_{T,k} &= v_{\mathrm{odo},k}+0.15\tanh(z_{v,k}),\\
\omega_{T,k} &= \omega_{\mathrm{imu},k}
 +0.020\tanh(z_{\omega,k})
 +0.005\tanh(z_{\mathrm{slow},k}).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:twin_bounded_corrections}
\end{equation}
The coefficients limit the speed correction to 0.15~m/s and the fast
and slow yaw corrections to 0.020 and 0.005~rad/s, respectively.
Using the corrected speed and yaw rate, the twin propagates planar
position and heading every 0.1~s by midpoint integration.
Training targets are reference-derived forward speed minus odometer speed
and reference-derived yaw rate minus IMU yaw rate. The slow target is the
training-only causal 30-s mean of the yaw correction; the fast yaw target
is the remaining correction. The frozen objective combines point-correction,
propagated-trajectory, persistent signed-yaw, slow-target, fast-target,
correction-magnitude, correction-mean, and slow-smoothness losses, with
weights \(1.0, 0.50, 1.0, 0.75, 0.25, 0.01, 0.25,\) and \(0.05\),
respectively. Training uses AdamW with learning rate \(10^{-3}\), weight
decay \(10^{-5}\), at most 25 epochs, early-stopping patience 5, batch size
8, and training/validation strides of 50/100 samples. Normalization is
fitted on training sequences, and validation sequences select checkpoints.
These settings define the source trajectories evaluated by the
service-relative framework.

All four services use the same position-and-heading stream from Twin V2,
but ask different questions of it. The local services assess motion over
1, 5, or 10 seconds, while global pose assesses current position and
heading. Keeping the twin and its inputs fixed makes this a controlled
comparison of service requirements. Table~\ref{tab:exp_services} lists
each service's horizon, physical tolerances, and maximum state age. All
contracts require physical, freshness, and joint satisfaction of at least
$\eta_s=0.80$, with an observable fraction of at least $\gamma_s=0.95$.

\subsection{Receiver Evaluation and Remediability}

The primary replay crosses source-state selection rates of 1, 2, 5, and
10~Hz with added delays of 0, 25, 50, 100, and 200~ms. Rate reduction
selects existing states from the saved 10-Hz stream; it does not interpolate
or create states. At each 10-Hz evaluation time, the receiver holds the
arrived state with the newest source timestamp. Its age is the difference
between the evaluation time and that source timestamp. Re-reading a cached
state does not reset its age. Arrival and age comparisons use integer
nanoseconds. Since the source and evaluation clocks run at 10~Hz and
arrivals at an evaluation instant are included, 25-, 50-, and 100-ms
delays produce identical receiver-held histories in this replay.

The original frozen bank computes derivative features with centered
gradients, which can require the next 10-Hz sample. Buffering that sample
adds one sample period to feature availability. The primary replay treats
each precomputed state as available at its source timestamp. A separate
timing sensitivity adds the corresponding 100~ms of availability delay
to the saved stream. A separately retrained backward-difference bank
uses only current and past samples. These analyses distinguish buffering
the same estimates from changing the estimator and its source trajectories.

The first 30~s provide sensor-only motion-context features for the
context-aware comparator and are excluded from scored outcomes. Local
services additionally require enough subsequent history to cover their
horizon. A sample is observable only if its required reference, receiver
state, synchronization, and history are available. Otherwise, it is
unobservable, not a pass or failure. Among observable samples, the evaluator
calculates physical, freshness, and joint satisfaction. A sequence--service
case qualifies only if all three satisfaction fractions are at least 0.80
and the observable fraction is at least 0.95.

The unit of generalization is the physical sequence. Three model seeds are
aggregated within each sequence before qualification fitting and statistical
summaries; seeds, timestamps, and delivery cells are not counted as
additional physical recordings. The doubly nested bank has ten outer folds.
For each outer test sequence, each of the other nine qualification
trajectories is generated by a pipeline excluding both its target sequence
and the outer test sequence from training, normalization, validation, and
checkpoint selection. The audited bank contains 30 outer-test trajectories
and 270 qualification-training trajectories across the ten folds.

The remediability ledger compares three fixed delivery points:
degraded (2~Hz/200~ms), practical (5~Hz/50~ms), and ideal-available
(10~Hz/0~ms). These rates provide representative low-to-high update settings, from sparse reporting at 1~Hz to the source stream's native 10~Hz. The source trajectory and service contract are unchanged
across the comparison; only the receiver's arrival history changes. A case
is \emph{delivery-remediable} if it fails under degraded delivery and
qualifies under ideal-available delivery. It is \emph{persistent under the
tested comparison} if it fails under both. It is \emph{already qualified}
if it passes under degraded delivery. The practical point tests whether
intermediate delivery restores a remediable case. Ideal-available delivery
forwards the full recorded 10-Hz stream without added delay or loss; the
source estimates retain their measured error.

To test which checks affect qualification, we compare four decisions on
the same cases. Source accuracy applies the physical limits to the
full-rate, no-added-delay stream. Receiver accuracy applies those limits
to the state held after the tested delivery. Separate checks require
physical and freshness satisfaction to meet 0.80 individually. The
complete contract requires their joint satisfaction to meet 0.80.
All four require adequate receiver observability, and the source
comparison also requires adequate source coverage.

\subsection{Held-Out Qualification Protocol and Comparators}
\label{subsec:heldout_protocol}

This analysis tests whether qualification results from earlier recordings can help an operator choose service--delivery settings to consider for a
new recording. A setting is one service paired with one update rate and delay. The service--delivery surface scores each setting by the fraction of development recordings that qualified under its contract.

We use ten physical sequences in a doubly nested leave-one-sequence-out
evaluation. In each outer fold, one sequence is held out for testing and
the other nine are used for development. The
recordings share the same robot, sensors, and state-estimation
pipeline, while covering several environments and showing
different error patterns. This gives the qualification rule a
common basis for learning from nine recordings, while leaving
meaningful variation for testing on the tenth. LOSO therefore
measures transfer to an unseen recording under the same study
setup. Each sequence has 80
service--delivery cells: four services crossed with 20 rate--delay
settings. Across the ten folds, this produces 800 held-out cells.
Because cells from the same sequence are related, the physical sequence,
not each individual cell, is the unit used for uncertainty estimates.

Table~\ref{tab:exp_comparators} compares the surface with six alternatives. Each uses a different
amount or form of information to rank service--delivery settings. For
the label-based methods, qualification labels come from the service
contracts defined earlier. The AoI-only method ranks settings using age
rather than those labels.

The surface and the two pooled scores are calculated directly from
development outcomes. The surface keeps both service and delivery
setting fixed. Service identity keeps the service fixed but ignores the
delivery setting. The common delivery score does the reverse: it keeps
the delivery setting fixed but averages across services. A higher score
means that a larger fraction of the relevant development cases
qualified.

AoI-only uses no physical-error labels to rank settings. It subtracts
the maximum receiver-state age from the service's allowed age; a larger
remaining margin ranks higher. Settings with the same margin receive
the same score, even if their physical accuracy differs.

The learned methods use one training row per recording--service--delivery
cell. Motion logistic fits one model across services, using service,
delivery, contract limits, and six sensor features. The service-specific
logistic and tree instead fit separate rate--delay relationships for
each service. For the logistic model, rate and delay are expanded into
the terms listed in Table~\ref{tab:exp_comparators}. The tree divides
the rate--delay settings into regions and scores each region by its
training pass fraction. If a fitting set contains only one qualification
label, that label is used as the score. Standardization and model fitting
are repeated using only the training sequences in each fit.

The six motion features use only the first 30~s of sensor data: mean and
95th-percentile absolute forward speed, mean and 95th-percentile
absolute IMU yaw rate, 95th-percentile absolute forward acceleration,
and the fraction of samples with absolute IMU yaw rate above
0.1~rad/s. No physical-reference error is used as a predictor.

For each outer test sequence, we choose a cutoff using only the other
nine sequences. We first take turns holding out each development
sequence. Its score is computed using a rule fitted to the other eight,
so no sequence is used to score itself. We combine these nine
cross-fitted scores and choose the cutoff whose acceptance is closest to
the requested target. Equal scores are kept together rather than split;
if two cutoffs are equally close, we choose the higher one. We then fit
the method using all nine development sequences and apply the selected
cutoff unchanged to the outer test sequence.

For example, when \texttt{building00} is the test sequence, each of the
other nine recordings is scored using a rule fitted to the remaining
eight. Those nine scores determine the cutoff. The rule is then fitted
using all nine development recordings and applied to
\texttt{building00}. The test sequence does not affect the cutoff or
fitting. Its accepted fraction can differ from the requested target
because its scores may differ from the development scores and tied
scores cannot be split.

At the 25\% requested-acceptance point, the surface, service identity,
and common delivery score each accept 200 of the 800 held-out cells.
Motion logistic accepts 199, service-specific logistic 203, and the
service-specific tree 200. AoI-only accepts 160 because its tied age
scores make 20\% the closest achievable held-out acceptance. These are
held-out counts: the target guides cutoff selection on development data
but does not prescribe how many test cells must be accepted. The
per-service counts use the same pooled cutoff; each service does not
receive its own separately selected cutoff.

For each method, false-qualification risk is the number of accepted
cells that fail the contract divided by the total number of accepted
cells. We report risk with the actual accepted count and coverage. If
no cells are accepted, risk is undefined. The held-out labels are used
only to evaluate the accepted cells, not to choose the cutoff. The
target range from 10\% to 50\% shows how risk changes as the screening
rule accepts more or fewer settings.

The resulting surface is an offline screening aid: it shows which
tested service--delivery settings have support in prior recordings,
so an operator can decide which settings to investigate or provision.
\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We begin with the difference between local motion and global pose, then
follow one recording through the receiver-side evaluation. We next
examine qualification across the delivery grid and identify which
failures improve when delivery changes. Component comparisons and
measured packet arrivals test the roles of receiver reconstruction and
delivery. Finally, we assess qualification on unseen recordings, vary
the service requirements and feature timing, and apply the evaluation
to UGV01 and MAGNET.

\subsection{Local and Global Fidelity Can Give Different Assessments}
\label{subsec:local_global_result}

Before considering network delivery, we examine the source trajectories themselves.
Figure~\ref{fig:local_global} compares two ways of describing their error. Each
point represents one of ten physical sequences, with the three model seeds averaged
within that sequence. The horizontal axis is 10-s translational relative-pose RMSE
($RPE_{10}$), which describes error in motion over a finite interval. The vertical
axis is the 95th-percentile global position discrepancy ($D_p$ p95), which describes
how far the reported position is from the physical reference.

The two measures rank the recordings differently. For example,
parking02 has a low $RPE_{10}$ of 0.097~m but a $D_p$ p95 of 22.345~m. Parking00
has a higher $RPE_{10}$ of 0.453~m but a lower $D_p$ p95 of 3.086~m. In other
words, a trajectory can represent recent motion well even when its
reported global position is far from the reference. A single trajectory
error measure would miss this difference, which is why the service
contracts test local motion and global pose separately.

\subsection{Worked Example: Applying the Framework to One Recording}
\label{subsec:worked_result}

The held-out \texttt{building00} recording shows how the framework
turns received states into service decisions. We keep its recorded
twin trajectory fixed and change the delivery condition. The receiver
therefore holds different states from the same source stream.

The example applies two service contracts to the same recording.
The local-1-s service evaluates whether the twin reproduces the
robot's motion over the preceding one second, using position and
heading tolerances of 0.10~m and $2^\circ$. The global-pose service
evaluates the robot's current position and heading, with tolerances
of 1.0~m and $5^\circ$. Their maximum permitted state ages are
0.6~s and 1.0~s, respectively. For both services, at least 80\% of
observable samples must meet the physical, freshness, and joint
requirements, and at least 95\% of evaluation samples must be
observable.

At each evaluation time, the receiver holds the newest state that has
arrived. The framework compares that state with the physical reference
and checks its age against the service limit. Joint satisfaction is the
fraction of observable samples that pass both checks. Coverage is the
fraction of evaluation samples with enough evidence to make those checks.

Figure~\ref{fig:worked_result} compares degraded delivery
(2~Hz/200~ms), practical delivery (5~Hz/50~ms), and
ideal-available delivery (10~Hz/0~ms). All samples are observable
(coverage is 1.000), and freshness satisfaction is 1.000 for both
services under all three conditions. Freshness is evaluated at 10-Hz sample instants, with packets arriving at an evaluation instant included. Under 2-Hz/200-ms delivery, the maximum saved age at those instants is 0.6 s, exactly the Local 1-s limit, which passes under the inclusive age rule. The continuous age between evaluation instants can approach 0.7 s, but is not part of the reported satisfaction calculation. The 30/6/4 ledger uses this 2-Hz condition; 1-Hz settings elsewhere in the delivery grid are separate.
Consequently, in this example,
joint satisfaction equals physical satisfaction. The distinction
between the services comes from the physical discrepancy of the
receiver-held states.

For local-1-s motion, joint satisfaction is 0.675 under degraded
delivery, 0.874 under practical delivery, and 0.958 under
ideal-available delivery. The required value is 0.80, so this service
fails under degraded delivery and passes under both practical and
ideal-available delivery. This is a delivery-remediable case, and
the practical condition restores qualification.

Global pose gives a different result on the same recording. Its joint
satisfaction is 0.165 under degraded delivery, 0.049 under practical
delivery, and 0.008 under ideal-available delivery. None reaches
0.80, so the global-pose service remains unqualified under all three
tested conditions, including ideal-available delivery. The local
service checks motion over one second, while the global service checks
position in the overall coordinate frame. More frequent states can
improve the motion comparison without correcting the source
trajectory's global position. On this recording, the states selected
under practical and ideal-available delivery yield lower global-pose
satisfaction than those selected under degraded delivery.

The reported values average three model-seed results for this one
physical recording.

\subsection{Service Qualification and Delivery Remediability}
\label{subsec:service_delivery_results}
\label{subsec:ledger_results}

We next ask whether the service differences seen in \texttt{building00}
also appear across the ten recordings and the full rate--delay grid.
Figure~\ref{fig:qualification_grid} shows the fraction of sequences
that qualify for each service and delivery setting. Satisfaction and
observability measurements are averaged across the three model seeds
within each sequence before qualification is determined. Each cell
therefore counts physical sequences.

Each heatmap cell answers how often a particular service qualifies at
one rate and delay. The local services qualify over different parts of
the grid, while global pose qualifies on fewer recordings across many
settings. A delivery configuration therefore has no single
service-independent qualification outcome.

    service and delivery condition. The horizontal axis shows added
    delay, and the vertical axis shows source-state selection rate.
    Color and cell labels indicate the qualifying fraction.}

The heatmaps cover the whole grid. The \emph{remediability ledger}
summarizes what happens when delivery improves for each
recording--service pair. We compare
degraded delivery at 2~Hz/200~ms with ideal-available delivery at
10~Hz/0~ms, keeping the source trajectory and service requirements
fixed. Across the 40 pairs, 30 fail under degraded delivery but qualify
under ideal-available delivery and are therefore
\emph{delivery-remediable}. Six fail under both conditions and are
\emph{persistent under the tested comparison}. The remaining four
already qualify under degraded delivery. All 40 pairs meet the
observability requirement.

At the intermediate practical condition of 5~Hz/50~ms, 27 of the 30
remediable cases already qualify. The same source trajectories produced
both outcomes, so the change follows from which source states arrive
and when. The six persistent cases still fail when the full recorded
stream is delivered without added delay.

Under the original age limits, freshness satisfaction is 1.000 at
the degraded operating point. The 30 remediable cases therefore fail
because their receiver-held states exceed the physical-discrepancy
limits, despite satisfying the age requirement. This illustrates why
delivery affects more than freshness: holding an older state can
change the evaluated position, heading, or relative motion enough to
alter service qualification.




\subsection{Contribution of the Evaluation Components}
\label{subsec:component_comparison}

Figure~\ref{fig:component_comparison} shows how qualification decisions
change when individual checks are removed. In the primary grid,
source accuracy alone passes
680 of 800 cases, while receiver-held physical accuracy passes 390.
The 290 cases that change status show why judging the source trajectory
alone can approve settings that fail after delivery.

Under the original age limits, the receiver-physical, separate-check, and
complete-contract decisions coincide. To expose the contribution of freshness,
the right panel uses the saved ordinary-LOSO study with a common 0.2-s age
limit under 5-Hz delivery, 50-ms delay, 20-ms jitter standard deviation,
and 1\% configured packet loss. Of its 40 sequence--service cases, source
accuracy passes 34 and receiver physical accuracy passes 31. Requiring
freshness as well reduces the count to 12. Requiring joint satisfaction
reduces it to eight.

The final four exclusions are local-1-s and local-5-s motion on

satisfaction fractions each exceed 0.80, but their joint fractions range
from 0.754 to 0.779. Each requirement is met often enough individually,
yet they are not met together often enough to qualify. The comparison
therefore identifies the decisions contributed by receiver reconstruction,
freshness, and their temporal overlap.

Figure~\ref{fig:threshold_sensitivity_result} shows how the ledger

\subsection{Feature-Timing Sensitivity}
\label{subsec:feature_timing_results}

We test sensitivity to the time at which source features become available.
Adding 100~ms of availability delay to the saved-state replay changes the
original-bank ledger from 30/6/4 to 31/6/3. A separate ordinary
ten-sequence LOSO retraining study using backward-difference features
produces a 28/8/4 ledger, with 26 of 28 remediable cases restored by
practical delivery. Mean ATE changes from 2.398 to 3.882~m, and mean
1-s RPE changes from 0.061 to 0.150~m. The remediability categories
remain present under causal feature construction, with changed counts
and trajectory errors. This ordinary LOSO study is separate from the
full doubly nested causal-feature evaluation reported in
Section~\ref{subsec:heldout_results}.

\subsection{Additional Evaluations Beyond i2Nav}
\label{subsec:beyond_i2nav}

We apply the framework to two additional datasets to examine its use
beyond the primary i2Nav study. The UGV01 evaluation tests the
position-only remediability ledger on another robot platform. The MAGNET
evaluation applies the physical-discrepancy and horizon analysis to
thermal forecasts.

\noindent\textbf{UGV01 position-only ledger:} Using position-reference
evidence, the three-recording ledger contains five already-qualified,
three delivery-remediable, and four persistent cases. The calibration
recording contributes two already-qualified and two remediable cases,
with none persistent. The two frozen-parameter holdout recordings
contribute three already-qualified, one remediable, and four persistent
cases. The 2-Hz rate is the highest source rate shared by all three
recordings; 1~Hz/200~ms provides a common lower-rate, delayed comparison.
Figure~\ref{fig:ugv01_result} shows all twelve recording--service cases
under degraded (1~Hz/200~ms) and ideal-available (2~Hz/0~ms)
delivery. The paired points show which individual cases cross the 0.80
joint-satisfaction requirement. Unlike an average across recordings,
this view exposes the three remediable cases and the four that remain
unqualified after delivery improves.
The ledger can therefore be evaluated even when
the available physical reference supplies position but not heading.

\noindent\textbf{MAGNET thermal evaluation:} MAGNET is a heat-pipe
experiment with paired physical measurements and digital-twin forecasts.
We use the released MAGNET heat-pipe measurements and forecast data
\cite{wilsdon2023magnet}. The analysis pairs ten forecasted
thermowell-temperature channels with the corresponding measured
temperatures by nearest timestamp. A 600-row forecast window is retained
only when at least 95\% of its predictions are present for every channel
and at least 99\% of its timestamps have a physical measurement within
0.25~s. Of 111 candidate windows, 23 pass these checks and are selected
greedily in time order without overlap.

For each channel, normalized error is the absolute
forecast--measurement difference divided by a robust scale computed from
the measured values in the retained windows: the 95th percentile minus
the 5th percentile, with a 1\% relative floor. Within each retained
window, we calculate the 95th-percentile normalized error over 60-,
300-, and 600-s evaluation horizons and compare it with the declared
normalized tolerances of 0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.10, 0.20, and 0.50. At each
horizon and tolerance, the physical-
discrepancy pass fraction is the number of window--channel pairs whose
95th-percentile error meets the tolerance, divided by the 230 evaluated
pairs (23 windows and ten channels). Forecast issue and arrival times are not
established for this analysis, so it evaluates thermal discrepancy over
time horizons rather than receiver-side freshness or delivery
remediability. Averaging the pass fraction over the six tested tolerances gives
0.943 for 60-s windows, 0.819 for 300-s windows, and 0.659 for 600-s
windows. Longer horizons therefore pass fewer physical-discrepancy
checks at the same tolerance.
Figure~\ref{fig:magnet_contract_surface} plots the pass fraction against
normalized tolerance for each horizon. Its vertical axis gives the
fraction of window--channel pairs that pass the physical-discrepancy
check, the component evaluated for MAGNET.

These studies extend two parts of the evaluation beyond i2Nav. UGV01
shows that the delivery ledger can be formed from position-reference
data on a second robot. MAGNET shows that a thermal forecast can be
judged against a quantity-specific tolerance over different horizons.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This work presents a receiver-side framework for deciding whether a
digital-twin state supports a specified service. It evaluates physical
discrepancy and freshness together, retains an unobservable outcome when
required evidence is missing, and compares delivery conditions with the
source trajectory held fixed. The qualification surface uses prior
sequences to screen tested service--delivery regimes; the paired ledger
shows whether a tested delivery change restores qualification.

Across the ten i2Nav sequences, the sampled-delivery ledger identified
30 delivery-remediable, six persistent under the tested ideal-available
delivery, and four already-qualified sequence--service cases. Practical
delivery restored 27 of the 30 remediable cases. Measured Linux
\texttt{tc netem} replay produced a 32/6/2 partition and restored 27 of
32 remediable cases. At a 25\% requested acceptance, the held-out surface
made four false qualifications among 200 accepted cases (2.0\%) with
centered features and 19 among 168 (11.3\%) on the separately trained
backward-difference bank. The service-specific logistic and tree
comparators produced similar risk, showing that the useful signal lies
in preserving the relationship between service requirements and delivery
conditions, rather than in one particular scoring rule. Together, these
results give operators evidence about which tested regimes merit
consideration and whether a delivery change can restore a service.

Analysis code and derived evaluation artifacts are available at
\url{https://github.com/CEISCA-VT/DigitalTwinDivergence}. The source
datasets are available from their cited providers.

Future work will extend the evaluation to additional physical systems and
network paths with explicit end-to-end state-availability timing, and
investigate how the resulting service--delivery evidence can inform
online resource management.
